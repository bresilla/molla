Magnetostatics — H(curl) Nedelec edge elements

Stage 46 brought H(curl)-conforming first-order Nedelec edge elements to molla, with a steady-state curl–curl solver for magnetostatics. The element basis lives in crates/molla-fem/src/nedelec_element.rs; the magnetostatics solver is in magneto.rs.

Why edge elements?

For PDEs in \( H(\mathrm{curl}) = { v \in L^2 : \mathrm{curl}, v \in L^2 } \) the natural conforming basis has tangential continuity across element interfaces — the tangential component matches on shared faces, the normal component is free to jump. This is exactly what magnetostatics (vector potential \( \mathbf{A} \)) and time-harmonic electromagnetics need: the curl operator is well-defined globally, but \( \nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} \) is not constrained and \( \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{A} \) has no physical meaning at material interfaces.

Nodal (H¹) elements over-constrain such problems by enforcing continuity of all components; they produce spurious “ghost solutions” that pollute the spectrum. Edge elements eliminate these by construction: the kernel of curl over the discrete H(curl) space is exactly the discrete gradient of H¹ scalar fields (the discrete de Rham complex).

The first-order Nedelec basis

On a tetrahedron with barycentric coordinates \( \lambda_0, \lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3 \) and constant barycentric gradients \( \nabla \lambda_k \), the lowest-order Nedelec basis (also called Whitney 1-forms) is one function per edge:

\[ \mathbf{N}_{(i, j)}(\mathbf{x}) = \lambda_i \nabla \lambda_j - \lambda_j \nabla \lambda_i \]

for edge \( e = (i, j) \). Properties:

  • \( \mathbf{N}_e \) is linear in \( \mathbf{x} \) (since \( \lambda_k \) are linear and the gradients constant).
  • The tangential component of \( \mathbf{N}_e \) along edge \( (i, j) \) is constant and integrates to 1 along that edge. Along every other edge it integrates to 0 — so the DoF value naturally represents the tangential-line integral of the field along the edge.
  • The curl is constant per element: \( \nabla \times \mathbf{N}_e = 2 \nabla \lambda_i \times \nabla \lambda_j \).

Edge orientation convention

Every unique mesh edge is stored canonically as [min, max] (see compute_edges). The corresponding global Nedelec basis function is the one that “points” from the lower-indexed vertex to the higher-indexed vertex.

When a cell’s local edge \( k \) (in the TET_EDGES ordering) has local endpoints \( (a, b) \) mapping to global vertices \( (v_a, v_b) \), the local basis \( \mathbf{N}_{\mathrm{loc}} = \lambda_a \nabla \lambda_b - \lambda_b \nabla \lambda_a \) points from local \( a \) to local \( b \). To express it in the global basis we apply an orientation sign:

\[ \mathbf{N}{\mathrm{glob}} = \mathrm{sign} \cdot \mathbf{N}{\mathrm{loc}}, \quad \mathrm{sign} = \begin{cases} +1 & v_a < v_b \ -1 & v_a > v_b \end{cases} \]

Element matrices computed in the local-edge basis are converted to the global-edge basis during assembly by multiplying entry \( (i, j) \) by \( \mathrm{sign}_i \cdot \mathrm{sign}_j \) — equivalently, absorbing the sign into the per-DoF scatter.

Element matrices

Mass — closed-form

#![allow(unused)]
fn main() {
pub fn compute_element_nedelec_mass(cell: &FemCell, vertices: &[Vec3]) -> Vec<Vec<Real>>;
}

The closed-form integral \( \int_K \lambda_p \lambda_q , dV = \frac{(1 + \delta_{pq})}{20} \cdot |K| \) combined with the constant gradients \( \nabla \lambda_k \) lets us write the mass entries directly without a quadrature loop:

\[ M_{e_1, e_2} = |K| \cdot \big[ c(j,l) \nabla \lambda_i \cdot \nabla \lambda_k

  • c(j,k) \nabla \lambda_i \cdot \nabla \lambda_l
  • c(i,l) \nabla \lambda_j \cdot \nabla \lambda_k
  • c(i,k) \nabla \lambda_j \cdot \nabla \lambda_l \big] \]

with \( e_1 = (i, j), e_2 = (k, l) \) and \( c(p, q) = (1 + \delta_{pq})/20 \).

Curl-curl stiffness

#![allow(unused)]
fn main() {
pub fn compute_element_curl_curl(cell, vertices, nu) -> Vec<Vec<Real>>;
}

Since the curl of a Whitney 1-form is constant per element:

\[ \nabla \times \mathbf{N}_{(i, j)} = 2 , \nabla \lambda_i \times \nabla \lambda_j, \]

the curl-curl integral collapses to

\[ K_{e_1, e_2} = 4 \nu \cdot |K| \cdot (\nabla \lambda_i \times \nabla \lambda_j) \cdot (\nabla \lambda_k \times \nabla \lambda_l) \]

— one cross product per entry, no quadrature. nu is the magnetic reluctivity \( \nu = 1/\mu \); for free space pass \( 1/\mu_0 \approx 7.958 \times 10^5 \) in SI units, or just 1.0 in non-dimensional units.

Magnetostatics solver

#![allow(unused)]
fn main() {
pub fn solve_magnetostatics_steady(
    mesh: &FemMesh,
    nu_per_cell: &[Real],
    j_source: &[Vec3],
    bcs: &[BoundaryCondition],
    max_iters: usize,
    tol: Real,
) -> Result<MagnetostaticsResult>;

pub struct MagnetostaticsResult {
    pub a: Vec<Real>,     // vector potential, one DoF per edge
    pub residual: Real,
    pub iters: usize,
}
}

Solves the steady-state curl–curl equation for the magnetic vector potential \( \mathbf{A} \):

\[ \nabla \times (\nu , \nabla \times \mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{J} \quad \text{in } \Omega, \quad \mathbf{n} \times \mathbf{A} = 0 \quad \text{on } \partial \Omega, \]

The “perfect conductor” boundary condition \( \mathbf{n} \times \mathbf{A} = 0 \) is the natural BC for the vector potential — in Nedelec DoFs it’s simply \( A_e = 0 \) for every edge on the mesh boundary (handled via strong Dirichlet).

Gauge fixing

The continuous curl-curl operator has a non-trivial kernel: every pure gradient \( \mathbf{A} = \nabla \phi \) is annihilated. On the discrete level the kernel is exactly the discrete gradient of P1 nodal scalars (lowest-order de Rham complex).

For a magnetostatics problem with a divergence-free source \( \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = 0 \) and fully-Dirichlet boundary on \( \partial \Omega \), the gradient kernel is killed at the boundary and what remains is gauge-equivalent. We don’t enforce Coulomb gauge \( \nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} = 0 \) explicitly — it’s not required to recover the physically meaningful \( \mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A} \).

In practice a tiny mass regularisation \( \varepsilon M \) (with \( \varepsilon \ll \nu / L^2 \)) is added to nudge CG towards a unique solution; the bias on \( \mathbf{B} \) is \( O(\varepsilon) \) and below the discretisation error.

Helper utilities

#![allow(unused)]
fn main() {
pub fn compute_curl_per_cell(mesh, map, a) -> Vec<Vec3>;
pub fn project_field_on_edges<F>(mesh, map, field: F) -> Vec<Real>;
pub fn integrate_field_along_edges<F>(mesh, map, field: F) -> Vec<Real>;
}

compute_curl_per_cell recovers \( \mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A} \) as one Vec3 per cell — used by the Magnetostatics viewer scene (#101) to render a B-field arrow at every cell centroid.

project_field_on_edges projects a vector field given as a closure \( \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) \) onto the Nedelec DoFs (tangential-line integrals along edges). Used for L²-projecting an analytic test field.

integrate_field_along_edges integrates a vector field along each mesh edge — useful for setting Dirichlet boundary values from a known \( \mathbf{A}_{\text{ref}} \).

Solenoid contract test

The Stage 46 test magnetostatics_solenoid_axial_field builds a cylindrical current loop, solves the magnetostatics system, and verifies that the recovered \( \mathbf{B} \) on the symmetry axis matches the analytic solenoid formula \( B_z = \mu_0 , I , n \) inside, decaying to zero outside, within ~5 % at the 16³ tet resolution.
